How it works

A personal research lab for stock portfolio strategies. It started as a crude "value investing" optimizer (Piotroski F-score filter + efficient frontier); this version rebuilds it on a foundation that can say whether any strategy actually works.

1. Universe

Every common stock and ADR listed on NASDAQ, NYSE and NYSE American, from NASDAQ's official symbol directory, refreshed daily. ETFs, funds, warrants, units, rights, preferred shares and notes are excluded. On any given day a stock is only eligible to hold if, using data up to that day, it traded, closed above $5, traded a median of over $1M a day for the past 60 days, and has at least a year of history.

Dead companies too. A universe built from today's listings silently drops every company that went bankrupt, was acquired or was delisted, which flatters backtests (survivorship bias). Tiingo's free ticker list names every stock that stopped trading, and Alpaca still serves their exchange history, so about 4,200 stocks delisted since 2016 are included (SVB, First Republic, Signature Bank, Bed Bath & Beyond, ...). A held stock that is acquired exits at its last price. One that falls to over-the-counter trading and never returns to an exchange exits at its last exchange price minus 30%, the average loss after such delistings found by Shumway (1997); SVB, for example, last traded at $106 before its halt.

2. Prices

Daily bars since 2016 from Alpaca's market data (the full consolidated tape), updated every trading evening after 8pm Eastern. Each day stores the prices that actually traded, plus returns adjusted for splits and dividends, so a stock's history never changes when it later splits.

3. Backtests

  • Walk forward, no peeking. At each rebalance a strategy sees data only up to that day's close. A test re-runs every strategy with all future data scrambled and requires its decisions to be identical.
  • Realistic execution. Trades happen at the next day's open. Costs are charged for crossing the spread plus market impact that grows with trade size relative to a stock's volume (at a $100k portfolio). Uninvested cash earns the 3-month Treasury bill rate.
  • Honest comparisons. Every strategy is scored against buy-and-hold SPY and an equal-weight portfolio of all eligible stocks: return, volatility, Sharpe ratio, drawdown, and alpha, beta and information ratio versus SPY.

4. Strategies

  • Baselines: buy-and-hold SPY, and equal weight (all eligible stocks, or the most liquid ones).
  • Mean-variance: the original optimizer's idea done properly. Return forecasts (the legacy ARIMA on prices, an AR(1) model on returns, or a plain trailing average) feed the efficient frontier over the 100 most liquid stocks.
  • Momentum: equal-weight the 20 biggest winners of the past year (skipping the last month), on the same 100 stocks or on every eligible stock.
  • Piotroski: the original optimizer's stock filter. From each company's annual reports (SEC XBRL filings, only as of the date each became public), nine signs of improving financial health are scored; the screen equal-weights companies scoring 8 or 9, and can also filter mean-variance's candidates. Banks and foreign filers lack the inputs and are skipped.

What the comparison shows so far. The forecasts add nothing over a plain trailing average. Plain momentum on the same 100 stocks earns the same return as mean-variance (about 25% a year since 2017), so the return is momentum; what the optimizer adds is risk control, with market-level beta and drawdowns instead of momentum's 1.35 beta and -50% drawdown, for a much better Sharpe ratio. Momentum across all eligible stocks does badly, so the effect lived in large, liquid names. The Piotroski quality screen, on the same 100 liquid stocks, beats equal-weighting them on return, Sharpe ratio and drawdown (with only about 11 holdings, so it is noisy); across all eligible stocks it barely helps, and in front of mean-variance it lowers return and risk together without improving the Sharpe ratio.

5. Signals

A backtest mixes the prediction with everything done to it afterwards (optimizer, costs, position limits), so a weak forecast can hide behind a good optimizer. The signal scoreboard tests predictions directly: each signal ranks the stocks at the end of every month, week or day, and the ranking is scored against the returns over the next period. It covers the forecast models and classic anomalies (momentum, short-term reversal, low volatility, Piotroski quality), which are the bar any new model has to clear.

What it shows. None of the return forecasts has real skill: the AR(1) model ranks stocks no better than their trailing average, and neither is distinguishable from luck, while the legacy ARIMA is reliably backwards. Mean-variance's returns came from holding recent large-cap winners in a decade that rewarded them, with the optimizer keeping risk in check. Only Piotroski quality and low volatility show skill, and only across all eligible stocks.

6. Lead-lag networks

Do some stocks consistently follow others a day or a week later? Each month the lab correlates every stock's returns with every other stock's previous-period returns over the past year (about 9 million pairs), keeps each stock's ten strongest leaders, and predicts it from their latest moves. A simpler version uses the biggest stocks as the only leader, the classic "large stocks lead small ones" effect.

Result: essentially nothing at daily or weekly resolution. Individual pair relationships do not persist: the strongest pairs one year keep their direction the next about half the time, a coin flip. The big-stock lead flips sign from year to year. Scored as daily signals, both networks show an IC of 0.003 to 0.008, a quarter to half of a stock's own one-day reversal and too small to survive the cost of trading daily. This matches research showing daily lead-lag in US stocks was arbitraged away decades ago; what is left lives at intraday speeds, which would need minute data.

Ripples after big moves: real, reliable, and immediate. Looking instead at events, when a large stock makes a sudden move of three standard deviations or more on heavy volume (about 14,000 times since 2017), its 20 most related stocks move the same way that day three times out of four, by about 1% on average. In daily data a small echo follows the next day (about 5 basis points, more in thinly traded stocks and after bad news), too small and too unreliable (51 to 52% hit rate) to trade. Minute bars for 2024 and 2025 (about 5,300 splashes) show why: by one minute after the move becomes visible, the related stocks have already made 97% of their move for the day. What follows is a few basis points with coin-flip reliability; only splashes at the open leave a small, fleeting catch-up (about 6 to 9 basis points within 15 minutes). The wave exists, but it arrives within seconds, a race for high-frequency traders.

Roadmap

  1. Foundation (this site): data, backtests, baselines, and the original optimizer rebuilt.
  2. Forecasting: attention-based sequence models using market-wide inputs, judged against the same baselines.
  3. Lead-lag network (done, see above): no usable effect in daily or weekly data.

Caveats

  • Survivorship bias, mostly fixed: about 4,200 dead stocks are included, but ones Alpaca has no exchange prices for are still missing, and dead companies have no F-score.
  • Trading costs are a model, not real fills.
  • This is research, not investment advice.
