How it works

A personal research lab for stock portfolio strategies: one continuous history of US stocks since 1998, an honest walk-forward backtest, two strategies, and a paper account trading the one in production.

1. Data

Every source is kept separately, as delivered and as rewritten into a common set of tables (prices, listings, corporate actions, filings, economic series). One reader merges them into a single continuous history, row by row: the most complete row wins, then the more trusted source. Everything downstream sees that one history and never which source a number came from.

A ticker is only a name a company used for a while (CADE has meant four different banks), so every security gets its own permanent id and each source's tickers are matched to it by date. Every evening the new data is fetched, merged, and the derived tables rebuilt: each stock's liquidity, its filings paired with the year before, and every input the models use at each month end.

2. Universe

Common stocks listed on NYSE, NASDAQ and NYSE American, including Canadian companies listed there; ETFs, ADRs, preferred shares, warrants and units are left out. On any day a stock can be held only if, using data up to that day, it traded, closed above $5, traded a median of over $1M a day for the past 60 days, and has a year of history. Companies that later went bankrupt, were bought or were delisted are all in the history. A held stock that is bought exits at its last price; one that falls off its exchange exits at its last exchange price minus 30%, the average loss after such delistings (Shumway, 1997).

3. Backtests

  • Walk forward, no peeking. At each rebalance a strategy sees data only up to that day's close. A test re-runs every strategy with all future data scrambled and requires its decisions to be identical.
  • Realistic execution. Trades happen at the next day's open, paying the spread plus market impact that grows with trade size against the stock's volume. Uninvested cash earns the 3-month Treasury bill rate.
  • From the start of the data. Every backtest starts once there is a year of history (January 1999) and is compared with funds anyone can buy over the same years.

4. The two strategies

Both rebalance at each month end, among the same candidates: the healthiest of the 400 most liquid stocks, health being the nine Piotroski measures (profitability, cash flow, improving returns, cash-backed earnings, falling debt, rising liquidity, no dilution, improving margins and turnover) as percentiles, averaged. There are no hard cutoffs: a stock's weight limit shrinks as it nears the edge of either list. Both weigh their candidates for the highest expected long-run growth (Kelly: expected return minus half the variance), at most 10% in any stock, and hold the lowest-risk mix instead in a bear market (the S&P 500 15% below its two-year high and below its 200-day average three month ends in a row).

  • Piotroski health (class 1, production; the paper account follows it): each stock's expected return is its trailing one-year return, and its risk the shrunk covariance of a year of daily prices.
  • Forecaster (class 2): each stock's expected return comes from the forecaster, nine terms fitted on all earlier months, rescaled by Grinold's rule; risk averages three covariances (prices, the forecaster's factors, its errors).

5. Paper account

Production trades an Alpaca paper account (simulated money) every month end, from the same code and data as its backtest; the paper page shows the two side by side.

Caveats

  • The strategies and their settings were chosen on the same history they are shown on; anything chosen that way looks better here than it will in the future.
  • Returns move a lot by chance: a point or two between two strategies over twenty years can be luck.
  • After Sharadar's history ends, filings come from SEC EDGAR, which reports some items (gross profit, SG&A, debt detail) for fewer companies.
  • Trading costs are a model, not real fills. This is research, not investment advice.

Built with
